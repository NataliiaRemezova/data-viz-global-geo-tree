GlobalGeoTree: Source Bias and Temporal Development

RQ2 - Source and Platform Bias, and RQ3 - Temporal Development, combined into one report

Research Questions

This report combines two related research questions, because they share the same data and the same key idea: GlobalGeoTree is not a neutral, evenly-collected dataset - it is built from thousands of different sources (mostly citizen-science apps), and that has consequences for how the geographic and temporal patterns in it should be read.

RQ2 - Source and Platform Bias. To what extent do data sources shape the observed geographic and taxonomic patterns in GlobalGeoTree?

  1. Which sources contribute the largest share of observations?
  2. Is the dataset dominated by citizen-science platforms such as iNaturalist?
  3. Do different sources cover different countries or regions?
  4. Are some sources associated with specific years, countries, or species groups?

RQ3 - Temporal Development. How has the number and distribution of tree observations changed from 2015 to 2024?

  1. Has the number of observations increased over time?
  2. Which years show the strongest growth or decline?
  3. Did geographic coverage expand over time?
  4. Are temporal patterns mainly driven by specific sources?

Both analyses are descriptive. Strong source-specific patterns should be read as evidence of recording and platform bias, not only as biological patterns in tree distributions.

What Is in “Other Source”?

Why we look at this: “Other source” is the second-largest group in this dataset, but unlike iNaturalist or Pl@ntNet it is not one platform - it is everything that didn’t match a recognised keyword pattern. Before using it in any chart, it’s worth showing what’s actually inside it.

Interpretation. “Other source” is not a single platform - it is a long tail of 2,330 individually named sources (national biodiversity portals, regional/government forestry or environmental-impact datasets, local citizen-science platforms such as Artportalen or Observation.org, and similar projects). Treat any chart that shows “Other source” as a group as a mixture, not as one consistent collection method - the countries and years where “Other source” is large are typically being driven by a handful of large national datasets rather than a single shared bias.

Source Concentration

Why we look at this: before comparing countries, years, or species, we first need to know whether the dataset is balanced across sources. If one source is extremely large, it shapes the whole dataset (RQ2 subquestions 1-2).

The source structure is highly concentrated: iNaturalist alone contributes 64.5% of observations, and iNaturalist plus Pl@ntNet together contribute 75.5%.

Interpretation. A few large platforms dominate the evidence base, so global summaries are strongly affected by the behaviour, geography, and taxonomy covered by those platforms specifically.

Temporal Development

Why we look at this: before anything more detailed, we need the basic shape of the data over time - is it going up, down, or flat (RQ3 subquestions 1-2) - and whether that shape is really about trees, or about one platform growing (RQ2 subquestion 4 / RQ3 subquestion 4).

Between 2015 and 2024, observations changed from 205,055 to 855,708, an overall change of 317.3%.

Interpretation. The strongest growth occurs in 2018 (+232,196), and the strongest decline in 2024 (-131,320). The 2024 figure should be treated with caution: if that year’s data was exported before the year finished, the apparent decline may shrink once a complete year is available.

Interpretation. If most growth is concentrated in one source group, the temporal trend is source-driven, not a neutral increase in global tree recording. This ranks absolute increases, so later years (when the whole dataset is bigger) have a built-in advantage - a percentage-growth version would be a useful follow-up.

Geographic Coverage Over Time

Why we look at this: more observations is not the same as broader observations - growth could just mean recording the same countries more intensively (RQ3 subquestion 3).

Interpretation. If country counts increase over time, GlobalGeoTree is expanding geographically; if observations grow without much country expansion, growth is concentrated in already well-covered places.

Countries and Regional Coverage

Why we look at this: a global source pattern can hide strong differences between countries - is each country’s evidence base built the same way (RQ2 subquestion 3)?

Interpretation. Countries differ both in volume and in source mix - a country dominated by iNaturalist is not directly comparable to one dominated by local inventories or unknown sources.

Interpretation. Low diversity means a country’s evidence base is essentially one platform’s user base, so any pattern found there is at higher risk of being platform-specific rather than biological.

Sources and Taxonomic Groups

Why we look at this: source bias can also be taxonomic - some platforms may record ornamental or easy-to-photograph families more often than others (RQ2 subquestion 4).

Interpretation. If a family is mostly recorded by one platform, its geographic pattern may partly reflect that platform’s user base rather than the tree’s real distribution.

Geographic Footprints of Sources and Time

Why we look at this: spatial clusters in the raw points may reflect user activity and platform coverage, not only real tree distributions (RQ2 subquestion 3 and RQ3 subquestion 3, viewed spatially).

Interpretation. Source maps and map snapshots distinguish two different processes: where each platform records data, and whether records over time spread into new regions or just intensify in existing ones.

Unknown Source Diagnostics

Why we look at this: unknown-source records reduce confidence in source-specific interpretation. This checks whether they’re concentrated in particular countries.

Interpretation. Unknown-source records are a metadata-quality issue, not necessarily a bias in one direction. Countries with a high unknown share have weaker evidence for any source-specific claim.

Overall Interpretation

This analysis shows that GlobalGeoTree is not source-neutral and not time-neutral:

  • A few platforms - mainly iNaturalist and Pl@ntNet - dominate the observation base.
  • “Other source” looks big in some charts, but it is a long tail of many different national/regional datasets, not one consistent collection method (see the dedicated section above).
  • Source composition varies by country, year, and taxonomic family, so geographic and taxonomic patterns are partly platform-coverage patterns.
  • The overall rise in observations from 2015-2024 is driven substantially by iNaturalist’s growth, and the apparent decline in 2024 may be a data-export artifact rather than a real drop.
  • Geographic coverage genuinely expanded (more countries, more grid cells over time), but that expansion is also unevenly distributed across platforms.

Main limitation. These figures describe patterns in the available records. They do not prove that any source is biased in a negative way - they show where source coverage is uneven and where interpretation needs extra care. For any downstream analysis, source composition should be reported alongside geographic, temporal, or taxonomic results, and key findings should ideally be re-checked with and without the largest citizen-science source.
